# Mission ARES — teacher guide

One page for a class or a family. Ages 7–12. One mission takes about 10 minutes; a second run (with "Skip the story" on the title screen) takes about 6. Plan 30–40 minutes with the discussion.

**The big idea:** on Mars nothing is free. Power, water and air are shared by everything, so every choice helps one thing and costs another. Real mission planners make these choices every day, using real data.

**Before playing (2 minutes):** "If you lived on Mars, what would you need to stay alive? Where would each thing come from?"

**While playing:** let the player choose alone. Watch the **Your choices** list on the crew tablet: every choice and what it caused stays there.

## Questions for each scene

### The crew tablet (landing)
1. Why does the tablet show Water and Air separately, if the oxygen maker makes air *from* water?
2. Which bar do you think will run out first? Check at the end: were you right?
3. A stowaway means 5 people instead of 4. What gets harder?

### Two jobs, one crew (the base)
1. Read both "If later" chips. Which price would you rather pay? Why?
2. Is there a choice with no cost at all? (There isn't: find the cost of each.)
3. Real missions also have to put jobs in order. What could happen if they got the order wrong?

### The greenhouse
1. More water and more light make a bigger harvest. Why not always give the most?
2. The water you give the plants is gone for the storm walls. How did you decide?
3. NASA tested lettuce grown in space for germs and nutrients. Why does it matter that space food is safe *and* healthy?

### The garage and the rover
1. The temperature is a real reading from the Perseverance rover. How is −83 °C different from the coldest place you know?
2. Asking Earth makes trips cheaper, but the reply takes minutes (the game shows today's real delay). When is waiting worth it? When is it not?
3. Why do scientists want lake-mud rock to look for signs of ancient life?

### The night
1. Even sleeping, the crew uses air. Where does new air come from, and what does it cost?
2. The solar panels give back only a little power at sunrise. What could make them give less? (Hint: the dust storm and the Opportunity rover.)

### The solar storm
1. The storm comes from a real eruption on the Sun (NASA DONKI). The forecast can be wrong. Did you pick the shield the forecast said, or a thicker one? Why?
2. The radiation card shows real readings from Mars: about 4 on a quiet day and 304 during a storm. How many times bigger is that?
3. Why use water for the walls? (Water is good at stopping radiation particles, and astronauts carry it anyway.)

### The result
1. Which choice cost you the most points? Would you change it?
2. Play again: the crop, the weather and the rock change. Try one different strategy and compare the scores.
3. Pick one "Back on Earth" card. How does solving a Mars problem help people on Earth?

## For older students (11–12) or a second session

- Open **NASA data** on the title screen. Pick one number and find where it comes from. What is real data, and what did the game makers choose?
- The storm's DONKI ID is shown on screen. Look it up on NASA's DONKI website (the result screen has the link).
- Design your own rule: "If ___ then ___ costs ___." Is there a choice that becomes free? How would you fix it?

## Words to know

- **Sol:** a day on Mars, 24 hours 39 minutes.
- **Radiation:** tiny fast particles from the Sun and space that can harm people. Mars has no magnetic shield and thin air to stop them.
- **Rover:** a robot car that drives on Mars.
- **Delta:** the fan of mud and sand a river drops where it flows into a lake.
- **Forecast:** a best guess about what will happen, made from measurements.
